Introduction

The purpose of this document is to document how we merged unique job titles drawn from the CES19 phone, CES19 web and CES21 surveys, coded with codes from Canada’s National Occupation Classification and to provide some diagnostics of the coding and merging. We undertook this at the request of the Consortium on Electoral Democracy.

While the initial request was only to code the occupation responses for the CES21 dataset, we included the 2019 surveys for two reasons. First, the CES19 surveys are closer in time to the Government of Canada’s 2021 National Occupation Classification than the 2016 NOC and, second, because of repeat entries, it was anticipated that this would not necessarily add a lot of extra work. That is to say, there are only so many ways respondents can enter the job title “nurse”, “teacher”, “carpenter”, etc.

Data Import

First, we imported the three files containing respondents and converted each variable containing occupation and job titles into all lower-case strings. Then we filtered only unique responses. As a result, Teacher and teacher became a single entry, but spelling mistakes were kept. So, we were left with a spreadsheet containing responses such as teacher and teahcher, and, more specific entries such as elementary teacher. This variable alone was exported into an Excel spreadsheet for coding by two research assistants.

Second, we imported the Excel spreadsheet that contains the unique occupation titles contained in the three surveys and the 4-digit and 5-digit NOC codes assigned by coders. More can and will be said about what the difference between the 4- and 5-digit codes, but basically it is simply that the 5-digit codes are more specific. This is an innovation in the NOC that emerged between 2016 and 2021.

As an example, someone providing the response “Kindergarden Teacher” could be classed with the 5-digit NOC code 41221 - “Elementary School and Kindergarten Teachers”. However, someone providing the response “Teacher” could be plausibly be an elementary or a secondary school teacher. The latter has the 5-digit code 41220. But both share the 4-digit code 4122.

But, presumably, researchers could make use of the information in the 4-digit code. As a result, after initially coding each response with a 5-digit code, we re-coded remaining responses with a 4-digit code and then copied the the first 4-digits of the 5-digit codes assigned to those in the first attempt.

As a result, the file with the NOC codes contains the unique, lower-case responses from the three survey files, a 5-digit NOC codes for some of the responses that were specific enough, and 4-digit NOC codes for those responses plus other responses that were somewhat more vague, but specific enough to assign to a 4-digit code.

read_excel(path=here("Data/occupations_coded.xlsx")) %>% 
  #Select only the columns with the unique job title and the NOC codes
  select("title", "NOC21_4", "NOC21_5") %>% 
  #Set 0s to be missing in the two columns containing the NOC code
mutate(across(2:3, function(x) car::Recode(x, "0=NA", as.numeric=T)))->
  # Store as object noc
  noc

There were 7904 unique responses to the questions soliciting job titles in the CES19 Phone, CES19 Web and CES21 Web. Of these, we were able to match 6213 NOC 5-digit codes and 6642 4-digit codes.

There are several reasons for why we cannot assign an NOC code to each job title.

  1. Many responses were broader than NOC codes permit. For example, some responses have more to do with a person’s economic sector or organizational function than their job title. Many respondents might respond “human resources” or “IT” which are hard to assign to an NOC code because it lacks skill level and task information. Other respondents provided something like “self-employed” which is not an occupation.

  2. In the web surveys, respondents who did not report they worked were given the opportunity to record their job title, leading to many responses such as “on medical leave” or “retired police officer”. Note, that the survey flow in the 2019 Phone survey was different; respondents were only asked their occupation if they previously answered that they had any paid employment. In the interests of consistency, we have not assigned any NOC codes to any responses where it is clear the respondent was not working (e.g. “retired teacher”, “cashier on sick leave”). Users interested in these variables should consider the possibility that there is still useful information that can be gleaned from searches of the open-ended text responses which we have refrained from coding into the NOC to avoid pre-judging other users’ responses.

Merge With Each File

The next step is to merge back the lower-case, unique job titles back into each CES file. We do this in the following way.

CES2019 Phone

First we import the 2019 CES phone survey.

ces19phone<-read_dta(here(file="Data/2019 Canadian Election Study - Phone Survey v1.0.dta"),encoding="utf-8")

We convert the occupation variable to lower case and merge with the noc dataset using the title variable in the noc dataset and the p52_lower variable in the ces19phone dataset as the key variables. So, this is taking any respondent in ces19phone who provided a job title and adding an NOC21-5 and NOC21-4 variable to that job title from the noc spreadsheet we created.

ces19phone %>% 
  #Convert p52 to lower case
  mutate(p52_lower=str_to_lower(p52)) %>% 
  #merge with noc keying on p52_lower and title
    left_join(., noc, by=join_by("p52_lower"=="title"))->ces19phone

Diagnostics 2019 Phone

In the following, we run some basic diagnostic tests to see how the merge funtioned. First, we wanted to see how the survey flow functioned in each survey, specifically, whether people who responded they were not technically employed actually had the opportunity to provide responses. So, first we converted the employment status variable into something useful. Specifically, we coded the employment status variable into a 3-category variable where respondents were either employed, retired or other. We will mirror this for the other surveys as well.

ces19phone %>% 
  mutate(employment=case_when(
    #If Q68 is between 0 and 4 employed
    q68>0 & q68< 4~ "Employed",
    # If Q68 is between 8 and 12 employed
    q68> 8 &q68<12~ "Employed",
    #If Q68 is 4 set Retired
    q68==4~ "Retired",
    #All other responses (e.g. student, family caregiver, don't know )
    # Set to other
    TRUE ~ "Other"
  )) %>% 
  #Set the factor levels for the new employment variable
  mutate(employment=factor(employment, levels=c("Employed", "Retired", "Other")))->
  #Save back into ces19phone
  ces19phone

ces19web %>% 
  mutate(pes19_occ_text_lower=str_to_lower(pes19_occ_text)) %>% 
  left_join(., noc, join_by("pes19_occ_text_lower"=="title"))->ces19web

Note, that in the online surveys CES19 and CES21, respondents were invited to report their job title even if their employment status was something other than having employment. That is to say, people who reported they were retired could also report their job title.

This lead to some confusion. On the one hand, it lead to many cases in the noc file of people saying their positions were retired police officer.

However, many, for whatever reason, did not mention they were retired in their response to the job title question. This information was unavailable to us when we coded the job titles. As a result, many respondents who were not working will have NOC 5- or 4- digit codes. Users should make use of the employment status variable prior to the NOC codes if they want to be sure they are capturing respondents who have paid employment.

In the next section we utilize this variable to examine the distribution of employed, retired or respondents in other situations (e.g. students) and their reported occupation titles.

It appears that we were much more successful at assigning NOC codes to the web surveys than to the NOC19 phone survey. It’s not entirely clear why that is. To be honest, I would have expected it to be different given that a phone interviewer would have had an opportunity to ensure respondents provided a bit more clear answers.

It is also clear that users should make strong use of the questions on employment status in combination with the NOC codes in the web surveys as respondents who were not employed often provided job titles, presumably from their past primary occupations. It’s not hard to imagine how one might want to make use of such information, but it would be very specific use cases.
